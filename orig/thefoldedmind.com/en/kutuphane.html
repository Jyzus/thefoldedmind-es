    Psychopathology

    Ten diagnoses, each with its brain circuits, findings from large studies, networks, neurotransmitters and treatment. In the atlas they open with four lenses.

    • Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity DisorderAttention, impulse control and reward circuits; frontostriatal and frontoparietal networks, dopamine and noradrenaline.ADHD
    • Autism Spectrum DisorderSocial perception, theory of mind, sensory processing and interoception circuits; imaging differences are small and group-level.ASD
    • Obsessive-Compulsive DisorderLoops through the orbitofrontal cortex, cingulate cortex, striatum and thalamus that "get stuck".OCD
    • DepressionReward, stress response and emotion regulation circuits; irritability and anhedonia can come first in adolescence.Depression
    • Generalized Anxiety DisorderThreat perception (amygdala), sustained anxiety about uncertain threat (BNST), bodily sensation (insula) and prefrontal regulation.GAD
    • Post-Traumatic Stress DisorderFear learning and extinction circuit: an overactive amygdala, with weak ventromedial PFC braking and weak contextual input from the hippocampus.PTSD
    • Bipolar DisorderEmotion regulation (amygdala, ventrolateral PFC) and reward circuits; swings between mania and depressive episodes.Bipolar
    • SchizophreniaIncreased striatal dopamine, the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex; positive, negative and cognitive symptoms.Schizophrenia
    • Borderline Personality DisorderEmotion regulation circuit: an overactive amygdala and an underactive DLPFC in response to negative emotion; mentalizing and social pain.Borderline
    • Tic Disorders and Tourette SyndromeSensorimotor cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop; the premonitory urge before tics is linked to the insula and supplementary motor area.Tics

    Neurotransmitter pathways

    The source nuclei, pathways and receptors of eight neurotransmitters, and the targets of medication. The book's main pathways are in the atlas's Pathways section too.

    • Glutamate PathwaysThe brain’s main excitatory signal: used by the projection neurons of the cortex, thalamus and hippocampus.3B
    • GABA PathwaysThe brain’s main braking signal: mostly in local interneurons; its long pathways are the outputs of the basal ganglia, cerebellum and amygdala.3B
    • Dopamine PathwaysThe signal of motivation, learning and movement: spreads along four main pathways; the shared target of stimulants and antipsychotics.3B
    • Serotonin PathwaysA modulator spreading from the raphe nuclei to the whole brain: mood, impulse control, sleep and pain; the target of SSRIs.3B
    • Noradrenaline PathwaysA wakefulness and attention signal spreading from the locus coeruleus; it launches the stress response and has an inverted-U relationship with…3B
    • Acetylcholine PathwaysThe cholinergic signal of attention, learning and wakefulness: from the basal forebrain to the cortex, from the septum to the hippocampus…3B
    • Histamine PathwaysOne of the chemical switches of wakefulness: from the tuberomammillary nucleus of the posterior hypothalamus to the whole brain.3B
    • Orexin (Hypocretin) PathwaysThe wakefulness stabilizer of the lateral hypothalamus: keeps the other wakefulness nuclei on together; its loss causes narcolepsy.3B

    Medications

    13 medications commonly used in child and adolescent psychiatry: site of action in the brain, the anatomy of side effects, pediatric evidence and approval status, interactions. In the atlas they open with the pathways they act on. For education only, not medical advice; no doses are given.

    Antidepressants (SSRIs)

    • FluoxetineSSRI · The only antidepressant significantly better than placebo in a network meta-analysis of child and adolescent depression (2016).
    • SertralineSSRI · An SSRI with large trials in pediatric OCD (POTS, 2004) and anxiety (CAMS, 2008); in these trials the best results came from combining it with CBT.
    • EscitalopramSSRI · The active enantiomer of citalopram; approved in the US for adolescent depression and for generalized anxiety in children and adolescents.

    Antidepressants (tricyclic)

    • ClomipramineTricyclic antidepressant · A tricyclic antidepressant that strongly inhibits serotonin reuptake; noradrenaline reuptake is inhibited by the drug and its active metabolite desmethylclomipramine.

    Antipsychotics

    • RisperidoneSecond-generation antipsychotic · The first large multicenter trial for irritability in autism (RUPP, 2002) used this drug; strong D2 and very strong 5-HT2A blockade.
    • AripiprazoleD2 partial agonist · Occupies nearly all D2 receptors without fully blocking them: usually does not raise prolactin, among the lower metabolic burdens in its class; keep akathisia in mind.
    • OlanzapineSecond-generation antipsychotic · Found effective in adolescent schizophrenia and bipolar mania; among the highest weight and metabolic burdens in pediatric comparisons…
    • QuetiapineSecond-generation antipsychotic · Thought to bind D2 loosely and let go quickly (fast-dissociation hypothesis): few extrapyramidal effects and little prolactin rise.
    • ClozapineSecond-generation antipsychotic · Used in treatment-resistant schizophrenia; low D2 occupancy and high 5-HT2 occupancy. The risks of agranulocytosis and myocarditis call for close monitoring.

    ADHD medications

    • MethylphenidateStimulant · Blocks the dopamine and noradrenaline transporters; the preferred first-choice medication for children and adolescents with ADHD in a network meta-analysis (2018).
    • AtomoxetineSelective noradrenaline reuptake inhibitor · Blocks only NET: raises noradrenaline and dopamine in the prefrontal cortex and leaves dopamine unchanged in the striatum and accumbens (animal data).

    Mood stabilizers

    • LithiumLithium salt · Used in bipolar disorder; found superior to placebo for mania at ages 7–17. Its narrow safety margin calls for blood level monitoring.

    Brain networks

    Large-scale brain networks: default mode, salience, executive control, attention and the rest; each network's anatomy, functions, development, clinical changes and references on its own page.

    • Seven Networks TogetherYeo 2011: every point of the cortex in its network's color3B
    • Default Mode NetworkThe network that switches on when we withdraw from the outside world: thinking about oneself, memories, imagining the future and understanding other…
    • Frontoparietal Control NetworkThe hub of flexible cognitive control: changes its connections with the other networks according to the task and regulates goal-directed behavior.
    • Multiple-Demand (MD) CoreA “general-purpose” core that activates together in almost any difficult task: the infrastructure of fluid intelligence and problem solving.
    • Salience / Ventral Attention NetworkThe network that notices what matters and turns attention to it: initiates switching between the other networks according to salient signals from the…
    • Dorsal Attention NetworkThe network that directs goal-driven attention: selects top-down what and where to look, and guides eye movements.
    • Somatomotor NetworkThe network that moves and feels the body; in the Yeo atlas it also includes auditory cortex. One of the most mature networks at birth.
    • Visual NetworkThe visual areas of the occipital lobe: the first steps from analyzing the image to perceiving objects and motion.
    • Limbic Network (Yeo)In the Yeo atlas, a network made of the orbitofrontal cortex and temporal pole; despite its name, it does not include the amygdala or hippocampus.

    Good to know

    The atlas is for education; it should not be used on its own for diagnostic or treatment decisions.

    The model is built from an average young adult brain; the child and adolescent brain differs in size and maturation. Developmental differences are in each card’s Development tab.

    Some small structures are modeled approximately, and pathways are schematic. Each region’s “In the model” note explains how it was made.
