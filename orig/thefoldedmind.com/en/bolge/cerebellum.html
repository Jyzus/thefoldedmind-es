Cerebellum

Cerebellum

Times and corrects movement, and applies the same computation to thought, language and emotion. Most of the brain’s neurons are here.

Anatomy

  • In the posterior cranial fossa, below the tentorium cerebelli; the vermis in the midline and two hemispheres. Anterior, posterior and flocculonodular lobes; folia.
  • Cortex: molecular, Purkinje and granular layers. Deep nuclei: dentate, interposed (emboliform + globose), fastigial.
  • Peduncles: superior (output — dentato-rubro-thalamic), middle (pontine input), inferior (spinal and olivary input).
  • About 80% of the brain’s neurons (granule cells) are here. Blood supply: SCA, AICA, PICA.

Substructures

11 substructures; each has its own page.

Surface parts (lobules)

  • Anterior Lobe (Lobules I–V) I–VThe sensorimotor anterior lobe of the cerebellum: the first somatotopic map of the trunk and limbs; regulates gait and posture.
  • Lobule VI VIA transitional lobule between sensorimotor and cognitive regions: active in movement, language and attention tasks.
  • Crus I Crus IThe cognitive core of the cerebellum: forms loops with prefrontal and parietal cortex and supports executive function and language.
  • Crus II Crus IIA posterior lobe region involved in social and cognitive processing: connected with mentalizing and executive networks.
  • Lobule VIIb VIIbA posterior lobe lobule between Crus II and lobule VIII, connected with attention and working memory networks.
  • Lobule VIII (VIIIa–VIIIb) VIIIThe lobule carrying the second sensorimotor body map; also connected with attention networks.
  • Lobule IX (Tonsil) IXA posterior lobe region connected with the default mode network; its hemispheric part is the cerebellar tonsil.
  • Flocculonodular Lobe (Lobule X) XThe vestibulocerebellum: regulates balance, eye–head coordination and holding gaze steady.
  • Posterior Vermis (Lobules VI–IX) VermisThe midline “limbic cerebellum”: linked to emotion regulation, eye movements and autonomic responses.

Inner structures (deep nuclei)

  • Dentate Nucleus DNThe largest deep cerebellar nucleus: carries the output of the lateral hemispheres via the thalamus to motor and prefrontal cortex.
  • Interposed Nucleus (Emboliform and Globose) IPThe output of the paravermal region: corrects limb movements on the fly; plays a role in conditioned eyeblink learning.

Functions

  • Motor coordination, balance and posture (vermis, flocculonodular lobe).
  • Timing and prediction of movement (internal models), motor learning.
  • Eye movements and adaptation of the vestibulo-ocular reflex.
  • Cognitive functions: the lateral posterior hemispheres (Crus I/II) form loops with the PFC — working memory, language, timing.
  • Emotional regulation (posterior vermis — the “limbic cerebellum”), social cognition.

Connections and chemistry

  • Pons (corticopontocerebellar input)
  • Inferior olive (climbing fibers)
  • Dentate → thalamus VL → motor and prefrontal cortex
  • Red nucleus
  • Vestibular nuclei

Development

Developmental course through childhood and adolescence.

  • Grows very rapidly in the last trimester and the first years; its volume increases by about 240% in the first year (Knickmeyer et al., 2008).
  • Peak volume comes later than in the cortex, in adolescence (Tiemeier et al., 2010).
  • Cerebellar injury after preterm birth has been linked to positive ASD screening (Limperopoulos et al., 2007).

Clinical

  • ASD: postmortem loss of Purkinje cells, vermis hypoplasia; early cerebellar injury has been proposed as one of the strongest risk factors (Wang, Kloth & Badura, 2014).
  • ADHD: reduced posterior-inferior vermis volume, timing difficulties.
  • Posterior fossa syndrome (cerebellar mutism): developed in 24% of 450 children after medulloblastoma surgery; preoperative brainstem involvement was the only risk factor. At one year, speech-language and cognitive problems and ataxia persisted in a large share (Robertson et al., 2006). A
  • Cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome (Schmahmann).
  • Joubert syndrome (the “molar tooth” sign), FASD (vermis), “cognitive dysmetria” in schizophrenia (Andreasen).
  • Negative symptoms in schizophrenia: the weaker the connectivity between the right DLPFC and the cerebellum, the more severe the negative symptoms; in 11 patients, 5 days of TMS to the vermis (lobule VIIb) restored connectivity, and the increase related to symptom improvement (Brady et al., 2019). A later sham-controlled study found no difference between arms (Basavaraju et al., 2021; 60 patients; connectivity increased, symptoms improved in both arms); the target is promising but not proven. C

Treatment

This region from the perspective of medication, psychotherapy and neuromodulation. Letter badges are evidence levels.

  • In posterior fossa syndrome: intensive speech-language therapy and neuropsychological rehabilitation; families are told that the mutism is temporary. B
  • Cerebellar TMS (vermis) for negative symptoms in schizophrenia is investigational (Brady et al., 2019). C
  • In ASD and ADHD the cerebellum is not a treatment target; occupational therapy for motor and timing difficulties.

Assessment

Neuropsychological tasks and measures that probe this region's function.

  • Finger–nose, heel–shin, rapid alternating movements (dysdiadochokinesia)
  • Tandem gait, Romberg
  • Rhythm and timing tasks; eyeblink conditioning

Lesions and damage

Clinical findings expected with damage or dysfunction.

  • Ipsilateral ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, nystagmus, scanning dysarthria, hypotonia.
  • Vermis: truncal ataxia.
  • Posterior lobe: executive, visuospatial, language and affective disturbances (flat affect, disinhibition).
