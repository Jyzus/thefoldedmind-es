Created by

Alperen Mert Çetin, MD

Resident in child and adolescent psychiatry

Like you reading these lines, I have long been drawn to psychiatry, psychology and neuroscience, and eager to learn. What drew me to these fields was the richness of abstract thought and theory, the countless questions waiting to be researched and, above all, the unknown of what goes on behind a person’s eyes. That unknown awakens curiosity and a desire to explore; in Panksepp’s language, a state of SEEKING.

In residency, the “why” and “how” questions that theory-based approaches raise but leave unanswered turned me toward the place where thought is produced: the organic side. Affective neuroscience, polyvagal theory, the Bayesian brain, mentalization… The more I read, the more the brain’s processes and regions turned into a heap of words that was hard to picture. That is when I realised how much a psychoanatomy atlas like this was needed.

My aim is not only to offer an atlas you can work with, but to keep a finger on the pulse of neuropsychiatry around the world, to follow the growing neuroscience community and to find a way to take part in its work.

The atlas is free. I would be glad to hear your suggestions, error reports and ideas for collaboration.

Hoping our paths cross in academia.

The book

A resident’s notebook

The Green Walnut on the CouchAffective Neuroscience and Psychoanalysis from Before Birth to AdolescenceTurkish original: Divandaki Yeşil CevizStill in progress; it will be made available here when complete.

When I first bought Panksepp’s 700-page book on affective neuroscience, I was full of excitement. As I read on, the translation and the technical detail became stifling. That is when I decided to draw up a curriculum for myself: seven or eight months of study through both theory and everyday life, in back-and-forth conversation with large language models, with plenty of free association. I gathered the notes I took after each lesson; enriched with metaphors and creative definitions, they grew into a 260-page resource I have enjoyed working with.

In these notes I tried to bring together the dynamic perspective of psychoanalysis and the mechanistic approach of neuroscience; starting, as far as I could, from the ideas of every major theorist, they are written around many topics and diagnoses. Rather than an academic book, think of it as a psychiatry resident’s notebook. I am still working on it, and when it is finished I will make it available here.

The atlas’s “In the book” layer comes from the notes’ Neuroanatomical Correlation chapter: select a region to see where it appears in the notes and which emotional system it relates to.

Happy reading.

The Green Walnut on the Couch1

I. Main Regions

PAG (periaqueductal gray)

RAGE · FEAR · PANIC/GRIEF · LUST · PLAY

Core role

A ring of gray matter in the midbrain surrounding the cerebral aqueduct (aqueduct of Sylvius), which connects the third and fourth ventricles. In Panksepp’s model, the shared alarm/action center of all the emotional systems — divided into columns (dorsomedial, dorsolateral, lateral, ventrolateral), each column running a different emotional-behavioral program.

Connections

Dorsal columns: RAGE (attack) and the active-defense end of FEAR. Lateral column: active escape, “fight/flight”. Ventrolateral column: freezing, the “playing dead” that overlaps with the dorsal vagal system. The separation call of PANIC/GRIEF and some components of LUST also pass through the PAG.

The PAG is the single most frequently mentioned structure in the book — and this is no coincidence: it is concrete evidence for Panksepp’s thesis that “emotions are subcortical”. That different emotional systems are coded in the same region, in different columns, shows that they are not separated by sharp borders but are neighbors in a compact architecture — this is the anatomical ground for why, in the clinic, overactivation of one system also triggers its neighbor (for example, intense FEAR leaping into RAGE).

II. Main Pathways

Opioid and endocannabinoid systems

not wanting (dopamine) but liking (hedonic tone)

What it carries

The non-dopamine half of the classic “reward” story. Endogenous opioids (endorphins, enkephalins) and endocannabinoids code how much an experience is “liked” (hedonic tone) rather than how much it is “wanted” — dopamine carries wanting, opioids/cannabinoids carry satisfaction.

Connections

Concentrated in the hedonic hot spots of the nucleus accumbens; also connected with the PAG, the PANIC/GRIEF circuit (soothing separation pain) and the PLAY circuit.

That wanting and liking are separate systems is the key to the book’s themes of addiction and insatiability: a person can intensely want something (a substance, a relationship, a behavior) (dopamine high) yet no longer take any pleasure from it (opioids low) — this is the neural explanation of the clinical picture “I want it so much but I’m never satisfied”.

II. Main Pathways

Fronto-limbic maturation pathway

vmPFC/OFC → amygdala and subcortical structures

What it carries

The process by which inhibitory projections descending from the PFC (especially the vmPFC/OFC) to the amygdala and other subcortical structures strengthen over the years after birth through myelination and synaptic reorganization. At birth this pathway is immature; its regulatory function becomes clear only toward adolescence — regulation is supplied from outside (by the caregiver).

Connections

The speed and degree of this pathway’s strengthening are directly linked to the quality of early CARE — the experience of safe, predictable regulation supports the maturation of this circuit.

This pathway is the direct anatomical counterpart of one of the book’s most fundamental theses — “regulation first comes from outside, then is internalized”. Almost every developmental transition in the book (a change of stage, the maturation of a defense mechanism, therapeutic change) means this pathway growing a little stronger.
Closing

Panksepp’s systems, Freud’s stages, Erikson’s crises and Mahler’s subphases, discussed one by one throughout the book, are in fact produced by different combinations of a shared, limited number of anatomical components (the PAG, amygdala, hypothalamus and a few cortical areas) and a handful of pathways that connect them.
