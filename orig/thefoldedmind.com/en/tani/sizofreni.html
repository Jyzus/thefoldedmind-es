Psychopathology · diagnosis

Schizophrenia

A psychotic disorder with hallucinations, delusions, disorganized thinking and behavior (positive symptoms), reduced affect and motivation (negative symptoms) and cognitive difficulties. Increased striatal dopamine function, the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex stand out in the brain.

Clinical picture (children and adolescents)

  • Early-onset schizophrenia (before age 18) is rare; onset before age 13 is very rare. Onset is often insidious and may be preceded by social withdrawal and falling school performance. B
  • Differential diagnosis: mood disorder with psychotic features, substance use, autism, organic causes. A

Circuits and networks

  • Caudate Nucleus and Putamen (especially the associative striatum): dopamine synthesis capacity and release are increased (Howes et al., 2012). A
  • Ventral Tegmental Area, Substantia Nigra: dopamine sources; overactivity of the mesolimbic pathway and underactivity of the mesocortical pathway is the classic model. B
  • Hippocampus: the largest volume difference in ENIGMA (d = −0.46) (van Erp et al., 2016); hippocampal overactivity is proposed to drive dopamine neurons. B
  • Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex: working memory and cognitive control; negative and cognitive symptoms. B
  • Superior Temporal Gyrus and Auditory Cortex (auditory cortex): auditory hallucinations. B
  • Thalamus, Amygdala, Nucleus Accumbens: smaller in ENIGMA; Globus Pallidus (pallidum) larger. A

Neurotransmitter systems

  • Dopamine (dopamine): in a meta-analysis of 44 studies, presynaptic dopamine function was increased (d = 0.79); a small increase in D2/3 receptors (d = 0.26) was not seen in unmedicated patients (Howes et al., 2012). A
  • Glutamate (NMDA receptor hypofunction hypothesis). B
  • The common action of antipsychotics is D2 receptor blockade. A

Imaging and neurobiological findings

  • ENIGMA-SZ (2,028 patients, 2,540 controls): smaller hippocampus (d = −0.46), amygdala (−0.31), thalamus (−0.31) and accumbens (−0.25); larger pallidum (+0.21) and lateral ventricles (+0.37) (van Erp et al., 2016). A
  • The aberrant salience model: dysregulated dopamine release lends meaning to neutral stimuli; delusions are an attempt to explain this experience (Kapur, 2003). Theoretical. C

Developmental course

  • Neurodevelopmental model: risk begins early in life, and symptoms appear in adolescence and young adulthood, coinciding with prefrontal maturation. B

Treatment and the systems it targets

  • Antipsychotics (D2 blockade): many second-generation antipsychotics are approved for adolescents; weight and metabolic side effects are monitored. A
  • In treatment-resistant early-onset schizophrenia, clozapine is the most effective option: in a double-blind trial of 39 adolescents, response was 66% with clozapine and 33% with olanzapine (Kumra et al., 2008); blood counts must be monitored. B
  • Psychosocial: family intervention, psychoeducation, cognitive behavioral therapy, school and work support. A
  • Long-acting injectable antipsychotics: In a meta-analysis of 137 studies, the risk of hospitalization or relapse is lower than with oral antipsychotics (RR 0.88 in randomized trials). The quality of observational studies limits interpretation. A
  • CBT for psychosis: In a meta-analysis of 34 studies the effect is small (overall symptoms −0.33; positive −0.25; negative −0.13) and shrinks further when sources of bias, especially masking, are controlled for. B
  • Early intervention services: In 10 randomized trials (2,176 patients), early intervention reduces treatment discontinuation and hospitalization compared with usual care; the advantage appears up to 24 months, except for general and depressive symptom severity at 18–24 months. A
  • Xanomeline-trospium: It targets M1 and M4 muscarinic receptors without blocking D2 receptors; the FDA approved it in 2024 for adults only. In a 5-week trial the PANSS score fell 8.4 points more than with placebo. B
