Psychopathology · diagnosis

Post-traumatic stress disorder

A disorder in which, after a serious traumatic event, re-experiencing (memories, nightmares, repetition in play), avoidance, negative changes in thinking and mood, and hyperarousal last at least one month. The fear learning and extinction circuit stands out in the brain: the amygdala overreacts to threat, while the ventromedial PFC does not brake it enough and the hippocampus does not tell contexts apart well enough.

Clinical picture (children and adolescents)

  • About 15.9% of trauma-exposed children and adolescents develop PTSD; the rate is higher after interpersonal trauma and in girls (32.9% in girls after interpersonal trauma, 8.4% in boys after non-interpersonal trauma) (Alisic et al., 2014; 43 samples, 3,563 people). A
  • In young children, re-experiencing may appear as traumatic play, frightening dreams and behavioral regression; adolescents show a picture similar to adults, sometimes with self-harm and substance use. B
  • Dissociative subtype: a sense of detachment from oneself or one's surroundings. B
  • Common co-occurring conditions: depression, other anxiety disorders, ADHD-like attention problems, sleep disorders. A

Circuits and networks

  • Amygdala: threat appraisal; in PTSD, an exaggerated response to threat cues. B
  • Medial & Frontopolar Prefrontal Cortex (ventromedial PFC): recall of fear extinction and braking of the amygdala; its activity is reduced in PTSD. B
  • Hippocampus: telling contexts apart ("this place is safe"); smaller in PTSD (Logue et al., 2018). A
  • Anterior Cingulate Cortex and Insula: salience and bodily arousal. B
  • Periaqueductal Gray, Hypothalamus & Pituitary, Locus Coeruleus: defense responses (flight, fight, freeze), the stress axis and noradrenergic arousal. B
  • Networks: increased activity in the Salience network (salience) network and reduced connectivity within the Default mode network (DMN) have been reported. C

Neurotransmitter systems

  • Noradrenaline (noradrenaline): hyperarousal and startle; the locus coeruleus. B
  • Stress axis (CRF, cortisol): regulation of the HPA axis is altered. B
  • Serotonin (serotonin): SSRIs are effective in adults; the evidence in children is weak. B

Imaging and neurobiological findings

  • ENIGMA-PGC (1,868 people, 794 with PTSD, 16 cohorts): in current PTSD the hippocampus is smaller than in trauma-exposed controls (d = −0.17); the amygdala difference (d = −0.11) is not significant after correction for multiple comparisons (Logue et al., 2018). A
  • These are group-level differences; imaging cannot diagnose an individual. A

Developmental course

  • Trauma during development can affect the maturation of prefrontal regulatory pathways and the stress axis; early intervention matters. B
  • Most trauma-exposed children do not develop PTSD; a supportive caregiver is protective. B

Treatment and the systems it targets

  • Trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (TF-CBT) is first line in children and adolescents; an individual participant data meta-analysis found it effective regardless of age, sex and trauma type (de Haan et al., 2024). A
  • Medication: in 131 children and adolescents, 10 weeks of sertraline did not beat placebo (Robb et al., 2010); SSRIs are not first choice in children but can be used for co-occurring depression and anxiety. B
  • Exposure aims to strengthen fear extinction learning (vmPFC → amygdala). B
  • EMDR: In a network meta-analysis of 32 trials in children and young people it is effective, though less so than individual trauma-focused CBT; the evidence is of moderate to low quality. B
  • Prolonged exposure: In 61 girls aged 13–18 with PTSD related to sexual abuse it beat supportive counseling, and the difference lasted 12 months; community clinic counselors delivered it. It uses fear extinction (the book's fronto-limbic pathway in the atlas). B
