Psychopathology · diagnosis

Obsessive-compulsive disorder

A disorder in which unwanted, distressing thoughts, images or urges (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors or mental acts performed to reduce that distress (compulsions) take up time and impair functioning. Cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loops running through the orbitofrontal cortex, cingulate cortex, striatum and thalamus stand out in the brain.

Clinical picture (children and adolescents)

  • Common themes: contamination and cleaning, fear of harm and checking, symmetry and a "not just right" feeling, counting, forbidden or repugnant thoughts. Children cannot always describe their obsessions; compulsions often draw in the family as well (family accommodation, reassurance seeking). A
  • Prevalence: about 2.3% lifetime and 1.2% in the past year (Ruscio et al., 2010). Onset is earlier in males. A
  • Onset: in a substantial share of patients, symptoms begin in childhood or adolescence; tic disorders and ADHD co-occur more often in early-onset OCD. B
  • Co-occurring conditions: anxiety disorders, depression, tic disorders and Tourette syndrome, ADHD. A

Circuits and networks

  • Orbitofrontal Cortex: appraisal of error and danger signals; in OCD, the "something is wrong" signal is mistuned. One of the most consistently activated regions in symptom-provocation studies. B
  • Anterior Cingulate Cortex: error monitoring; in OCD the error-related brain response (ERN) is enlarged, also shown in children. B
  • Caudate Nucleus and Putamen: habit and action selection; compulsions becoming automatic. Right caudate metabolism decreases in those who respond to behavior therapy or medication (Baxter et al., 1992). B
  • Thalamus: the return arm of the loop; in ENIGMA, thalamic volume was larger in unmedicated children and adolescents (Boedhoe et al., 2017). A
  • Globus Pallidus and Hippocampus: in ENIGMA, adults had a larger pallidum and smaller hippocampus; pediatric and adult patterns differ (Boedhoe et al., 2017). A
  • Inferior Parietal Lobule and Superior Parietal Lobule: in ENIGMA, the parietal cortex was thinner in both children and adults; effects are small (Boedhoe et al., 2018). A
  • Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex: cognitive flexibility and inhibition; difficulty stopping compulsive behavior. B
  • Networks: an imbalance between the Frontoparietal control network (cognitive control) and Salience network (salience, error) networks and the limbic network has been proposed. C

Neurotransmitter systems

  • Serotonin (serotonin): the efficacy of SSRIs and clomipramine is the key evidence; OCD needs higher doses and longer treatment than depression. A
  • Dopamine (dopamine): in SSRI non-responders, especially with co-occurring tics, adding a low-dose antipsychotic helps. A
  • Glutamate: the main excitatory transmitter of the cortico-striatal loop; trials of glutamate-modulating drugs are ongoing, evidence is limited. C
  • Heritability: about 47% in a Swedish twin study (Mataix-Cols et al., 2013). A

Imaging and neurobiological findings

  • ENIGMA-OCD subcortical (1,830 patients, 1,759 controls): larger thalamus in unmedicated children (d = 0.38); larger pallidum and smaller hippocampus in adults (Boedhoe et al., 2017). A
  • ENIGMA-OCD cortex (1,905 patients, 1,760 controls): thinner inferior and superior parietal cortex in children, thinner inferior parietal cortex in adults; small effect sizes (d = −0.10 to −0.33). Differences were more widespread in medicated patients (Boedhoe et al., 2018). A
  • In an ENIGMA study comparing ADHD, autism and OCD, small differences in cortical surface area were shared by all three disorders; no OCD-specific structural difference was found (Boedhoe et al., 2020). A

Developmental course

  • That brain findings differ between pediatric and adult OCD suggests the disorder affects the loops of the developing brain at different stages (Boedhoe et al., 2017). B
  • A substantial share of childhood-onset OCD diminishes or remits by adulthood; in others it runs a chronic course. B

Treatment and the systems it targets

  • First line: cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) with exposure and response prevention. In children and adolescents (ages 7–17, 112 patients), remission rates at 12 weeks: CBT + sertraline 53.6%, CBT alone 39.3%, sertraline alone 21.4%, placebo 3.6% (POTS, 2004). A
  • Medication: FDA-approved drugs for children are sertraline (age 6 and up), fluoxetine (7), fluvoxamine (8) and clomipramine (10); they inhibit serotonin reuptake. In the Turkish product information (KÜB), clomipramine is indicated at ages 10–17 only for OCD. A
  • For treatment-resistant adult OCD there are deep brain stimulation (targeting the ventral capsule and ventral striatum) and transcranial magnetic stimulation options; they are not used in children. B
  • Family involvement: reducing family accommodation of compulsions is part of treatment. B
  • Antipsychotic augmentation: In an adult meta-analysis, aripiprazole was effective in the short term and risperidone had a small effect; quetiapine and olanzapine did not differ from placebo. The authors advise cautious use and review at 4 weeks. Long-term risks are kept in mind. B
