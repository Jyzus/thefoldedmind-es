Chapter 8.1The SEEKING system

Panksepp’s most central system — most of the other six run on top of it. The mesolimbic and mesocortical dopamine pathway (VTA → nucleus accumbens → PFC). It is not tied to a particular goal — where hunger says “find food”, SEEKING only says “search, explore, engage”. Panksepp’s most original finding: dopamine produces seeking, not pleasure. An animal whose nucleus accumbens is stimulated electrically presses the lever again and again but is not happy — it looks greedy, restless, searching. Wanting (dopamine, SEEKING) and liking (the opioid system, the hedonic hot spots of the accumbens) are separate systems. In addiction the two come apart: the dopamine system is over-sensitised — the “search, find” signal is very strong — but the opioid system has become desensitised, and the old pleasure does not come. The person both wants a great deal and is never satisfied.

The history of this debate goes back to Olds and Milner’s (1954) discovery that rats would keep pressing for hours a lever that stimulated particular regions of their brains. Panksepp reinterpreted this circuit, long called the “pleasure centre”, as a system of anticipation and seeking rather than pleasure. Berridge and Robinson (1998) demonstrated the same distinction experimentally as “wanting” (dopaminergic incentive salience) and “liking” (hedonic impact mediated by opioids and endocannabinoids). Schultz and colleagues (1997) showed that dopamine neurons respond not to the reward itself but to unexpected reward, that is, they carry a kind of reward prediction error. This finding is the bridge that connects SEEKING to Friston’s language: dopamine is the signal that “there is something to learn here”.

Chapter 8.2SEEKING — neuroanatomical pathway

The centre is the ventral tegmental area (VTA) — the source of the dopaminergic cell bodies. The mesolimbic pathway runs from the VTA to the nucleus accumbens (motivation, the “want” signal); the mesocortical pathway runs from the VTA to the PFC (goal-directed planning, attention). The lateral hypothalamus and the periventricular system synchronise SEEKING with homeostatic needs such as hunger and thirst. The neurochemical carrier is dopamine; glutamate excites the VTA, GABA brakes it. Suppressing the system: high-dose antipsychotics (D2 blockade), chronic stress (lasting changes in the firing pattern of VTA dopamine neurons and in dopamine release); strengthening it: methylphenidate, amphetamine derivatives (they block dopamine reuptake; amphetamines also increase release).

Chapter 8.3Clinical correlate

In depression VTA–accumbens activation is low — anhedonia is SEEKING going out, not merely a loss of pleasure. In Parkinson’s disease the loss of dopaminergic neurons directly affects motor SEEKING (initiating movement). Over-activity of the same system is also a clinical picture. In mania and psychosis excessive SEEKING appears as giving meaning to everything, limitless plans and high energy; in Friston’s language, too much precision is given to unimportant signals. That impulse-control problems with gambling, shopping and sexual behaviour can be seen in Parkinson’s patients taking dopamine agonist drugs (Chapter 11) is another example of SEEKING being “opened too far” from outside.

Panksepp's seven systems

The first language: Panksepp’s primary-process emotional systems

In animal studies, Jaak Panksepp showed that electrical or chemical stimulation of particular subcortical brain regions brings out emotional behaviour patterns that are the same every time, consistent, and conserved across species. He defined these patterns as seven primary-process emotional systems and wrote their names in capital letters so that they would not be confused with everyday words. For Panksepp these systems are not only behaviour programmes but also the source of raw subjective experience, that is, of feeling. Animals experience stimulation of these regions as reward or punishment; they learn to switch the stimulation on and off.

The core claim

There is something older beneath the cortex. Although the newborn’s PFC is not yet functional, the baby cries when separated, calms when fed and brightens in play. These emotions come from the brainstem and subcortical structures — evolutionarily conserved systems that can work independently of the cortex. Panksepp calls them the neurobiological counterpart of the id.
