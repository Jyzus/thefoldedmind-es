Chapter 8.7The PLAY system

SEEKING and CARE being tested together, again and again, within safe limits. Neuroanatomically, the upper parts of the PAG and the parafascicular thalamus, closely linked with the opioid and endocannabinoid systems. Its evolutionary function: to simulate social and motor rules without the risk of real consequences. “Self-handicapping” — the stronger side holds itself back, roles change in turn — teaches the social negotiation of real life (how much strength may be used, when to stop). PLAY is a training gym for the development of the PFC and the orbitofrontal cortex.

In rats the most marked form of PLAY is rough-and-tumble play. Panksepp showed that during play and when tickled, rats make ultrasonic calls around 50 kHz, that these calls are rewarding, and that the animals turn towards the hand that tickles them; he interpreted these calls as “rat laughter” (Panksepp and Burgdorf, 2003). Pellis and Pellis (2009), by showing that in rats deprived of play experience before adolescence the structure of prefrontal neurons and later social flexibility develop differently, gave experimental content to the “training gym” metaphor.

In the human child, play develops from solitary play to parallel play, and from there to associative and cooperative play (Parten, 1932). Symbolic (pretend) play, which grows rapidly richer between three and five years, requires the child to tell the real from the imagined and to hold both at once; this is the healthy form of Fonagy’s “pretend mode” and is closely linked to the development of theory of mind.

Chapter 8.9PLAY — neuroanatomical pathway

The centre is the parafascicular thalamus and the dorsomedial diencephalon — the regions that initiated play behaviour in Panksepp’s animal studies. The periaqueductal grey (PAG) is linked with play sounds and laughter-like vocalisations (in rats, ultrasonic 50 kHz calls). The opioid and endocannabinoid systems carry the reward value of play; oxytocin eases the pairing of social play. The cerebellum and the basal ganglia support the motor coordination and rule-learning side of play; through repeated use these loops shape the capacity of the OFC and PFC for social and behavioural regulation.

Chapter 8.10The clinical correlate of PLAY

PLAY is in a mutually suppressing relationship with the RAGE and FEAR systems — high FEAR or RAGE activation puts out PLAY directly, which is why a child in an unsafe environment cannot play. In autism the social-reciprocal side of PLAY (role play, negotiating rules) stays weak while repetitive or solitary play can be preserved — this distinction is linked to the OFC’s capacity to process social reciprocity. A clinically useful rule: if a child cannot play in the playroom, it is safety, not play, that needs to be assessed first. The absence of play is usually not a “motivation” problem but a sign that FEAR or PANIC/GRIEF is suppressing PLAY. The return of play, like the return of SEEKING in depression, is one of the most reliable early signs of recovery.

Panksepp's seven systems

The first language: Panksepp’s primary-process emotional systems

In animal studies, Jaak Panksepp showed that electrical or chemical stimulation of particular subcortical brain regions brings out emotional behaviour patterns that are the same every time, consistent, and conserved across species. He defined these patterns as seven primary-process emotional systems and wrote their names in capital letters so that they would not be confused with everyday words. For Panksepp these systems are not only behaviour programmes but also the source of raw subjective experience, that is, of feeling. Animals experience stimulation of these regions as reward or punishment; they learn to switch the stimulation on and off.

The core claim

There is something older beneath the cortex. Although the newborn’s PFC is not yet functional, the baby cries when separated, calms when fed and brightens in play. These emotions come from the brainstem and subcortical structures — evolutionarily conserved systems that can work independently of the cortex. Panksepp calls them the neurobiological counterpart of the id.
