Hypothalamus & Pituitary

Hypothalamus et hypophysis

The manager of internal balance: regulates temperature, hunger, sleep, stress and puberty through hormones and the autonomic nervous system.

Anatomy

  • Floor and lower lateral walls of the third ventricle; below the thalamus (hypothalamic sulcus). The lamina terminalis and optic chiasm lie in front, the mammillary bodies behind.
  • Nuclei: suprachiasmatic (SCN — circadian clock), paraventricular (CRH, oxytocin, vasopressin), supraoptic (ADH), arcuate (appetite, GnRH network, dopamine), ventromedial (satiety, defense), lateral (orexin/hypocretin), preoptic (temperature, sleep — VLPO).
  • Pituitary: anterior lobe (hormonal control via portal vessels), posterior lobe (axonal release of oxytocin and ADH).
  • Blood supply: branches of the circle of Willis, superior hypophyseal artery.

  • Medial Preoptic Nucleus MPOThe nucleus at the front end of the hypothalamus: involved in parental care, sexual behaviour and temperature regulation.
  • Suprachiasmatic Nucleus SCNThe brain's master circadian clock: uses light information from the retina to align sleep, feeding, hormone and body temperature rhythms with the…
  • Supraoptic Nucleus SONThe large-cell nucleus above the optic tract: carries vasopressin and oxytocin directly to the posterior pituitary and maintains water balance.
  • Anterior Hypothalamic Area AHAThe area between the preoptic area and the tuberal region: involved in temperature regulation and in defensive or aggressive behaviour.
  • Paraventricular Nucleus PVNThe command post of the stress axis: starts the HPA axis with CRH; with oxytocin and vasopressin it contributes to bonding and water balance…
  • Dorsomedial Nucleus DMHA relay that turns clock information from the SCN into rhythms of sleep, feeding and cortisol.
  • Ventromedial Nucleus VMHThe large nucleus of the tuberal region: regulates defensive and aggressive responses, satiety and glucose balance.
  • Arcuate Nucleus ARCThe appetite and hormone centre at the base of the hypothalamus: houses the hunger and satiety neurons, the dopamine neurons that restrain prolactin…
  • Lateral Hypothalamus LHHome of the orexin and MCH neurons: a broad area, crossed by the medial forebrain bundle, that feeds wakefulness, feeding and reward seeking.
  • Tuberomammillary Nucleus TMNThe brain's only source of histamine: supports wakefulness; the target behind the sleepiness and appetite gain caused by many psychiatric drugs.
  • Posterior Hypothalamic Nucleus PHThe nucleus above the mammillary bodies: involved in arousal and sympathetic responses; an experimental deep brain stimulation target in refractory…
  • Mammillary Body MBThe round eminences at the back end of the hypothalamus: receive information from the hippocampus through the fornix and pass it to the anterior…
  • Pituitary Gland PituitaryThe gland that turns hypothalamic commands into blood signals: ACTH, prolactin, growth hormone and, from the posterior lobe, oxytocin and vasopressin.

Functions

  • Homeostasis: body temperature, hunger–satiety, thirst and water balance.
  • Sleep–wake and circadian rhythm (SCN, orexin, VLPO).
  • Neuroendocrine axes: HPA (stress–cortisol), HPG (puberty), HPT (thyroid), growth hormone, prolactin.
  • Top-level control of the autonomic nervous system.
  • Basic motivated behaviors: feeding, sex, defense; attachment and social bonding (oxytocin, vasopressin).

Connections and chemistry

  • Pituitary (portal system, neurohypophysis)
  • Amygdala and BNST (stria terminalis)
  • Hippocampus (fornix)
  • Brainstem autonomic nuclei and PAG
  • Retina → SCN (retinohypothalamic tract)
  • Orexin, histamine (tuberomammillary), oxytocin, CRH

Development

Developmental course through childhood and adolescence.

  • Onset of puberty: reactivation of pulsatile GnRH release (kisspeptin).
  • Circadian phase delay in adolescence: melatonin is released later, a tendency to go to bed late.
  • Early-life stress changes the programming of the HPA axis.

Clinical

  • Anorexia nervosa: hypothalamic amenorrhea (HPG suppression), HPA activation.
  • Antipsychotic-related weight gain (H1, 5-HT2C) and hyperprolactinemia (tuberoinfundibular D2 blockade).
  • Narcolepsy type 1: 85–95% of the orexin (hypocretin) neurons in the lateral hypothalamus are lost; neighboring MCH neurons are spared, and gliosis suggests a degenerative (probably autoimmune) process (Thannickal et al., 2000). Onset is often in childhood and adolescence; it can be mistaken for an attention problem or depression. B
  • Prader-Willi syndrome (hyperphagia), craniopharyngioma (hypothalamic obesity, behavior change).
  • Hypothalamic hamartoma: gelastic (laughing) seizures, precocious puberty, behavior problems.
  • Early puberty in girls is associated with increased risk of depression and anxiety.
  • Oxytocin in autism: in 290 children aged 3–17, 24 weeks of intranasal oxytocin did not improve social function more than placebo (SOARS-B; Sikich et al., 2021). Off-label use is not supported. B

Treatment

This region from the perspective of medication, psychotherapy and neuromodulation. Letter badges are evidence levels.

  • Narcolepsy (children): sodium oxybate is FDA-approved from age 7 for cataplexy and daytime sleepiness (2018); pitolisant (an H3 receptor inverse agonist that increases histamine release) is FDA-approved from age 6 for daytime sleepiness (2024). Both compensate downstream of orexin loss. A
  • Prader-Willi syndrome: diazoxide choline (extended release) is the first FDA-approved drug for hyperphagia, from age 4 (March 2025). A
  • Monogenic and syndromic obesity: setmelanotide (MC4R agonist) is FDA-approved from age 2 for POMC, PCSK1 or LEPR deficiency and Bardet-Biedl syndrome, and from age 4 for acquired hypothalamic obesity (e.g. after craniopharyngioma). Direct targeting of the hypothalamic satiety pathway. A
  • Central precocious puberty: GnRH agonists halt pubertal progression by desensitizing the pituitary. B
  • Antipsychotic monitoring: weight, fasting glucose, lipids and prolactin; hyperprolactinemia comes from tuberoinfundibular D2 blockade.
  • Oxytocin: ineffective for social function in autism (Sikich et al., 2021). B

Assessment

Neuropsychological tasks and measures that probe this region's function.

  • Endocrine assessment: cortisol rhythm, prolactin (antipsychotic monitoring), thyroid
  • Actigraphy and sleep diary
  • Growth chart, Tanner pubertal staging

Lesions and damage

Clinical findings expected with damage or dysfunction.

  • Diabetes insipidus, hypo/hyperthermia.
  • Hyperphagia and obesity or cachexia; sleep disorders.
  • Rage attacks (ventromedial), endocrine deficiencies, memory impairment (mammillary bodies).
