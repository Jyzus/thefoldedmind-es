Chapter 3.2Separation calls

Panksepp’s first major finding in this field concerned separation calls. The separation vocalisations of puppies, guinea-pig pups and chicks separated from their mothers fell markedly with very low doses of morphine and rose with the opioid antagonist naloxone (Panksepp et al., 1978). Oxytocin and prolactin had a similar soothing effect. A bold analogy was born from this: social attachment is a kind of “addiction” to the brain’s own opioids, and separation a kind of withdrawal. The analogy is still productive for understanding the striking phenomenological similarities between grief and substance withdrawal (restlessness, pain, sleeplessness, longing).

Chapter 3.2PANIC/GRIEF — neuroanatomical pathway

The centre is the periaqueductal grey (PAG) — in the brainstem, the source of the separation alarm. From the PAG it extends to the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), the thalamus, the amygdala and the anterior insula. The ACC is where physical pain and social pain meet — the pain of separation and physical pain use the same circuit. Neurochemical carriers: CRF, glutamate, substance P. Suppressing the system: opioids and oxytocin — that is, the CARE system directly inhibits PANIC.

In Panksepp’s electrical stimulation maps, the line that most reliably triggers separation calls is: dorsal PAG → dorsomedial thalamus → the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST), preoptic area and ventral septum → anterior cingulate cortex. The main substances that excite the system are CRF and glutamate; those that suppress it are μ-opioids, oxytocin and prolactin. In humans, in the “Cyberball” experiment of Eisenberger and colleagues (2003), activation of the dorsal ACC in participants excluded from a virtual ball game was proportional to the felt pain of exclusion: the best-known evidence for the idea that social pain uses a neural substrate shared with physical pain.

Chapter 3.2Psychoanalytic counterparts

The observations Bowlby and James Robertson made of young children admitted to hospital (and Robertson’s 1952 film A Two-Year-Old Goes to Hospital) described three phases of the response to long separation: protest (crying, searching, anger), despair (withdrawal, apathy, quiet sorrow) and detachment (visible indifference towards the attachment figure). Panksepp and Watt (2011) link the painful quality of depression to the second phase of this sequence: the protest phase is PANIC/GRIEF activation charged with CRF and glutamate; when it lasts, the system passes into a “despair” mode together with the shutting down of the opioid and dopamine systems.

Panksepp's seven systems

The first language: Panksepp’s primary-process emotional systems

In animal studies, Jaak Panksepp showed that electrical or chemical stimulation of particular subcortical brain regions brings out emotional behaviour patterns that are the same every time, consistent, and conserved across species. He defined these patterns as seven primary-process emotional systems and wrote their names in capital letters so that they would not be confused with everyday words. For Panksepp these systems are not only behaviour programmes but also the source of raw subjective experience, that is, of feeling. Animals experience stimulation of these regions as reward or punishment; they learn to switch the stimulation on and off.

The core claim

There is something older beneath the cortex. Although the newborn’s PFC is not yet functional, the baby cries when separated, calms when fed and brightens in play. These emotions come from the brainstem and subcortical structures — evolutionarily conserved systems that can work independently of the cortex. Panksepp calls them the neurobiological counterpart of the id.
