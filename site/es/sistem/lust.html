From the book

Passages translated from the author's book The Green Walnut on the Couch (Turkish original: Divandaki Yeşil Ceviz; a book in progress; Introduction, chapters 3, 8–11).

Chapter 11.1What LUST is

One of Panksepp’s seven basic emotional systems — the subcortical system that organises sexual desire and reproductive behaviour. It is directed by particular nuclei of the hypothalamus (the medial preoptic area — MPOA, the ventromedial hypothalamus — VMH) and activated by the sex hormones (testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone).

A developmental detail: the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis becomes active briefly in the first months after birth (mini-puberty); this transient rise in sex steroids plays a part in the maturation of the reproductive organs and probably in the sex-dependent differentiation of the brain. The axis is then suppressed centrally and stays largely silent until puberty. When the book says “LUST is silent in childhood”, it is this long period that is meant.

Chapter 11.3Neuroanatomical connections

These hypothalamic nuclei receive input from the medial nucleus of the amygdala (smell and pheromone information — through the vomeronasal system), from the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST — the bridge between chronic stress and sexual motivation) and from a ventral subregion of the PAG specific to LUST. That the PAG takes part here too explains, if we recall Solms’s thesis on consciousness, why sexual arousal is felt as so raw, preconscious and bodily. The dopaminergic SEEKING system (VTA–nucleus accumbens) overlaps directly with LUST — the “wanting” component of sexual desire works like a subset of SEEKING; this is why dopamine agonist drugs (in some Parkinson’s treatments) sometimes produce hypersexuality as a side effect.

In a large cross-sectional study of Parkinson’s patients, impulse-control disorders with gambling, shopping, eating and sexual behaviour were shown to be markedly more frequent among those taking dopamine agonists (Weintraub et al., 2010). This side effect is like a behavioural “natural experiment” of SEEKING being over-stimulated from outside.

Panksepp's seven systems

The first language: Panksepp’s primary-process emotional systems

In animal studies, Jaak Panksepp showed that electrical or chemical stimulation of particular subcortical brain regions brings out emotional behaviour patterns that are the same every time, consistent, and conserved across species. He defined these patterns as seven primary-process emotional systems and wrote their names in capital letters so that they would not be confused with everyday words. For Panksepp these systems are not only behaviour programmes but also the source of raw subjective experience, that is, of feeling. Animals experience stimulation of these regions as reward or punishment; they learn to switch the stimulation on and off.

The core claim

There is something older beneath the cortex. Although the newborn’s PFC is not yet functional, the baby cries when separated, calms when fed and brightens in play. These emotions come from the brainstem and subcortical structures — evolutionarily conserved systems that can work independently of the cortex. Panksepp calls them the neurobiological counterpart of the id.
