Chapter 10The FEAR system

The most common psychiatric disorders of childhood are the anxiety disorders. This chapter argues that anxiety is not one thing: FEAR, directed at a threat out in the world, and PANIC/GRIEF, directed at the breaking of a bond, are different systems; they produce different histories and need different treatment. The chapter also looks at how fear generalises, how it is passed from generation to generation, and how the brain goes “downstairs” as a threat comes closer.

Chapter 10.1Telling FEAR from PANIC/GRIEF

Different evolutionary tasks: PANIC/GRIEF is the response to a bond breaking, to something missing (absence, emptiness, loss); FEAR is the response to the presence of a concrete threat outside (a predator, a height, sudden movement) — not “the absence of the good” but “the presence of the bad”. They also separate clearly in neuroanatomy: the centre of FEAR is the central nucleus of the amygdala — the main station that takes the threat signal and turns it directly into a behavioural and autonomic response; PANIC/GRIEF works through a region of the PAG of its own and the ACC.

Chapter 10.5The PAG’s division into subregions — a bridge to Damasio

That the PAG assigns different regions to different emotional systems (the dorsal parts to RAGE and FEAR, the lateral and ventral parts to LUST, PANIC/GRIEF descending through a dorsal region of its own) supports the somatic marker thesis: each emotion produces its own distinct bodily and motor scenario (a package of behavioural and autonomic output); the insula and the ACC read these different bodily signatures (the pattern of heart rate, muscle tone, breathing) and label them as distinguishable emotions — because the source already comes from separate circuits.

Chapter 10.7The hippocampus and the loss of context

The hippocampus codes the context of a threat (where, when). If hippocampal function is impaired by chronic cortisol, FEAR becomes independent of context — it can be triggered anywhere, at any time. This is a shared mechanism of generalised anxiety disorder and PTSD.

Context also decides whether fear is extinguished. Extinction does not erase the old fear learning; it writes over it a new, context-bound learning of “safety”. That is why a fear extinguished in the therapy room can come back in another setting (the renewal effect; Bouton, 2004). The practical consequence: exposure work should be repeated in different settings, with different people and within everyday life.

Panksepp's seven systems

The first language: Panksepp’s primary-process emotional systems

In animal studies, Jaak Panksepp showed that electrical or chemical stimulation of particular subcortical brain regions brings out emotional behaviour patterns that are the same every time, consistent, and conserved across species. He defined these patterns as seven primary-process emotional systems and wrote their names in capital letters so that they would not be confused with everyday words. For Panksepp these systems are not only behaviour programmes but also the source of raw subjective experience, that is, of feeling. Animals experience stimulation of these regions as reward or punishment; they learn to switch the stimulation on and off.

The core claim

There is something older beneath the cortex. Although the newborn’s PFC is not yet functional, the baby cries when separated, calms when fed and brightens in play. These emotions come from the brainstem and subcortical structures — evolutionarily conserved systems that can work independently of the cortex. Panksepp calls them the neurobiological counterpart of the id.

For education only, not medical advice. Circuits and chemistry come from the table in the book’s Introduction (built on Panksepp, 1998 and Panksepp and Biven, 2012); (+) marks neurochemicals that excite the system, (−) those that suppress it.
