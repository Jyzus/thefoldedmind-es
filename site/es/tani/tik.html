Tic disorders and Tourette syndrome

Neurodevelopmental disorders with sudden, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic movements or sounds (tics); in Tourette syndrome, multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic last for more than a year. The sensorimotor cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop stands out in the brain; the "premonitory urge" that precedes tics is linked to the insula and supplementary motor area.

Clinical picture (children and adolescents)

  • Prevalence of Tourette syndrome was 0.52% in a meta-analysis of 21 population studies (Scharf et al., 2015); transient tics are much more common. Compared with national diagnosis data, this suggests that about half of affected children may never have been diagnosed. A
  • Tics usually begin at ages 4–6, are most severe in early adolescence and decrease toward adulthood in most children. B
  • Premonitory urge: an uncomfortable bodily sensation before a tic that is relieved by the tic. B
  • Common co-occurring conditions: ADHD and OCD. A

Circuits and networks

  • Putamen and Caudate Nucleus: sensorimotor and habit loops; children with a smaller caudate had more severe tic and OCD symptoms in adulthood (Bloch et al., 2005; 43 children). B
  • Supplementary Motor Area and Pre-SMA (supplementary motor area) and Primary Motor Cortex: preparing and executing tics. B
  • Insula and Anterior Cingulate Cortex: the premonitory urge; a network that activates before the tic (Bohlhalter et al., 2006; 10 adults). C
  • Thalamus (centromedian-parafascicular complex) and Globus Pallidus, Internal Segment: deep brain stimulation targets in resistant cases. C
  • Cerebellum: active at the moment a tic begins. C

Neurotransmitter systems

  • Dopamine (dopamine): dopamine blockers reduce tics; hypothesis of differences in striatal dopamine function. B
  • Noradrenaline (noradrenaline): α2 agonists (guanfacine, clonidine) are used especially with co-occurring ADHD. B

Imaging and neurobiological findings

  • Inverse relationship between childhood caudate volume and adult symptom severity (Bloch et al., 2005); small sample, not a tool for individual prediction. B

Developmental course

  • Tics wax and wane; they increase with stress, fatigue and excitement. In most children they decrease markedly by the end of adolescence. B

Treatment and the systems it targets

  • Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT): in 126 children (ages 9–17), clear improvement was 52.5% with CBIT versus about 18.5% with supportive therapy (Piacentini et al., 2010). Tic awareness and competing-response training. A
  • Medication: aripiprazole is FDA-approved for Tourette syndrome at ages 6–18; α2 agonists and other antipsychotics are also used. A
  • In severe, resistant adult cases, deep brain stimulation (thalamic CM-Pf or GPi); evidence rests on small studies. C

Guideline recommendations (Europe)

  • NICE has no guideline for tic disorders (October 2026). The reference guideline in this field is the 2022 update of the European Society for the Study of Tourette Syndrome (ESSTS). Summarised in our own words; no doses are given.
  • Psychoeducation is the first step regardless of symptom severity (Andrén et al., 2022).
  • If psychoeducation is not enough, behavioural treatments are first-line: habit reversal training or its expanded form, the Comprehensive Behavioral Intervention for Tics (CBIT), and exposure and response prevention; cognitive and third-wave approaches are not recommended on their own (Andrén et al., 2022).
  • Medication when behavioural treatment is not effective, available or feasible; the most evidence is for dopamine-blocking agents, preferably aripiprazole because of its side-effect profile; tiapride, risperidone and, especially with coexisting ADHD, clonidine and guanfacine may also be considered (Roessner et al., 2022).
